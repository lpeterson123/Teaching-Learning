The Six Pillars of Research-Informed Teaching and Learning

The six pillars — Managing Cognitive Load, Retrieval Practice, Spacing, Interleaving, Feedback, and Metacognition — are not six separate rules to comply with. They are one integrated system, and they enter your planning at different moments.

Six Moments, Six Pillars

Each pillar enters your planning at a different moment. Tap a moment to jump to that pillar.

The definitionCognitive load is the total mental effort a task demands of working memory. Because working memory is small and fixed, our job is to cut the load that's wasted, manage the load that's necessary, and protect the load that actually builds understanding.

Working memory is the mental workbench where all new learning is assembled, and it holds roughly four items of genuinely novel information at once. Everything a student learns has to pass through it. When it's full, learning stops.

Three kinds of load

The bad — reduce it
Extraneous

Load from poorly designed instruction — clutter that doesn't help the learner reach the goal. Confusing slides, text read aloud verbatim, a diagram split across two pages.

The necessary — manage it
Intrinsic

The inherent difficulty of the material — how many pieces have to be held in mind at once. Manage it through sequencing and chunking.

The good — optimize it
Germane

Effort that builds a durable mental framework: comparing concepts, solving a problem after instruction, explaining an idea in one's own words.

What it is not

  • Not "make it easier." We're cutting wasted effort, not rigor — intrinsic difficulty is the curriculum.
  • Not "always tell, never let them struggle." Productive struggle is essential; the point is minimizing unsupported struggle.
  • Not permanent scaffolding.

Five tips from Dr. Jared Cooney Horvath

  • Stop popcorn reading. Students focused on sounding right aren't thinking about the text.
  • Filter notes for meaning. Move students toward notes on connections, not transcription — handwriting helps because it's slower.
  • Use images one at a time. Point to parts of a chart, or build it piece by piece, rather than showing the whole gist at once.
  • Reset attention cheaply. Say a student's name, or shift modality — listening to writing.
  • Print anything over three pages. The physical page uses spatial memory to aid retrieval.

The definitionRetrieval practice is the act of pulling information out of memory rather than putting it back in — and it is a learning event, not just an assessment.

When a student retrieves something, they don't read it off a shelf — they reconstruct it, and that effortful reconstruction strengthens both the memory and the routes back to it. This is the testing effect, and it's the answer to the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve.

Four things retrieval does

  • It improves retention — shifting effort from passive review to active reconstruction.
  • It improves transfer — retrieving in varied contexts builds the ability to apply knowledge to novel problems.
  • It diagnoses gaps — giving student and teacher immediate, low-stakes information on what's actually known.
  • It's hard, and that's the point — struggling to recall beats re-reading, re-noting, or listening again.

What it is not

  • Not high-stakes testing. The benefit comes from frequent, low- or no-stakes retrieval students aren't afraid of.
  • Not a grading tool. When retrieval is used as practice, don't attach a meaningful score — reframe it explicitly as practice, not judgment.
  • Not re-reading, highlighting, or re-copying notes. Among the weakest strategies available, though they feel like the strongest.

The definitionSpacing means distributing learning over time rather than massing it into a single block — returning to material after enough delay that recalling it takes real effort.

Its opposite is massed practice — cramming. Cramming works briefly and creates an illusion of mastery: the information sits in working memory, feels completely available, and was never encoded into long-term storage. It will get a student through Monday's quiz. It will not survive the final.

Key concept: desirable difficulty

What spacing introduces is desirable difficulty — the slight struggle of recalling something just as it begins to fade. That struggle is not a design flaw; it's the mechanism.

Why it's genuinely hard for students

  • It requires organization — from us in sequencing lessons and units, from them in building a study plan.
  • It feels frustrating and slow — precisely because it depends on some forgetting having occurred.
  • Students won't choose it on their own. Without incentive, they need to be coached into it — just as we were.
Domain IV · Practice & Memory Consolidation Domain III · Curriculum Sequencing Brain: Hippocampus — consolidation depends on time, repetition, and sleep
Go deeper — key sources
Ebbinghaus (1885) on the forgetting curve; Cepeda et al. (2006), the definitive meta-analysis of distributed practice; Kang (2016), a favorite here for focusing on using the time we already have rather than adding time; The Learning Scientists' one-page Spaced Practice guide.
The definitionInterleaving means mixing different topics, problem types, or skills together within a single practice session, so students must work out which approach a problem calls for.

Its opposite is blocked practice — a whole session devoted to one skill. Blocked practice works for short-term acquisition, but students never practice the actual skill of deciding: cumulative assessments and real-world problems don't arrive with labels. The gain from interleaving is discrimination — the ability to look at an unlabeled problem and select the right tool.

Interleaving frequently produces worse performance during practice and better performance on a later test. Tell students it will feel harder — that's the mechanism, not a failure.

What it is not

  • Not random variety. Interleave related, confusable material — similar statistical tests, parallel grammar forms, mass versus weight.
  • Not for first acquisition. Students need some initial blocked practice to grasp a procedure before mixing helps. Block to acquire; interleave to consolidate and discriminate.

In the classroom

  • Mix the practice set — three problem types in rotation rather than ten of the same.
  • Make reviews cumulative and mixed, not organized by unit.
  • Design in a spiral — across an activity, a lesson, a unit, or a student's four years here.
  • Name the difficulty. Tell students it will feel harder and they'll slip more in practice.

The definitionFeedback is information that tells a learner where they are, where they're going, and what to do next — and it only counts as feedback if the student acts on it.

Task and process, not the person. "Here's what this paragraph needs, and here's the approach that will get it there" — not comments about the student. The loop has to close: feedback is finished only when the student has responded and you've verified the response. If there's no time built in to act on a comment, the comment was for you, not for them.

What it is not

  • Not more comments. Volume isn't the goal; uptake is.
  • Not one-directional. If it doesn't change what the student does next, it isn't finished.
  • Not only the teacher's job. Structured self-assessment and peer feedback both count.

In the classroom

  • Star and wish — one specific thing done well, one specific, actionable next step.
  • Anchor it to success criteria — share the rubric first, so feedback points at a target students already see.
  • Close the loop in class — feedback → revise → verify, with response time built into the period.
  • Whole-class feedback — address a common error once, to everyone.
  • Use "wise feedback." Pair high standards with explicit assurance: "I'm giving you this critique because the standard is high and I'm confident you can reach it."
  • Normalize error — meet a wrong answer with "walk me through your thinking."

The definitionMetacognition is thinking about one's own thinking: monitoring what you actually understand, then acting on that judgment to change what you do next.

Two parts, both required: monitoring (judging your own understanding) and control (acting on that judgment). The problem this pillar solves is calibration — students are routinely poor judges of what they know, and the weakest students tend to be the most overconfident. Left alone, they trust what feels easy over what's effective.

What it is not

  • Not vague reflection journaling. It matters only when it changes what a student does next.
  • Not innate or fixed. It's teachable and worth explicit instruction, inside your subject, on real work.
  • Not "any questions?" Everyone nods. Use a quick retrieval that reveals the truth instead.

In the classroom

  • Predict & Reflect — students predict their quiz score, then reflect on why it differed afterward.
  • Knowledge Surveys — before a unit, students rate confidence in each objective to prioritize study.
  • Retrieval Diagnosis — low-stakes brain dumps show students exactly what they actually know.

Judgments of Learning: alongside an assessment, ask for a quick, ungraded confidence rating on each response. Paired with feedback, this sharpens a student's metacognitive awareness enough to target future study with real precision.
